## Why Paper Logs Aren't Enough Anymore

A paper log records what a guard writes. It can't show where the guard was, whether checkpoints were reached, or whether entries were made in real time. That leaves fire marshals, insurers, and owners trusting a document they can't verify.

The stakes are real. Under commonly cited provisions in NFPA 101 and IFC 901.7, an alarm out for more than 4 hours, or sprinklers out for more than 10 hours, in a 24-hour period generally requires evacuation or an approved fire watch. For hot work, OSHA 1910.252 requires the watch to continue at least 30 minutes after work ends. Verification is how a property demonstrates it met those requirements.

## Why Geofencing Is the Key Layer

GPS shows movement, but movement through a parking lot isn't the same as checking the fourth-floor mechanical room. Geofenced checkpoints tie the patrol to the areas that matter. For a sprinkler impairment, that might be the unprotected zones. For hot work, it might be the work area, the floor below, and the space behind a wall. Defining checkpoints during the site briefing turns the patrol route into something measurable.

## Designing Checkpoints During the Site Briefing

Before the watch begins, the provider reviews affected areas, guard count, access instructions, patrol route, and emergency procedures. That's the moment to define checkpoints. Good checkpoint sets usually include every impaired zone, each stairwell serving those zones, mechanical and electrical rooms, storage areas with high fire load, and any hot work location along with the space below and behind it.

## What Realistic Records Look Like

A verified fire watch record typically includes the property, guard, shift times, round-by-round timestamps, areas and checkpoints reached, observations, actions taken, photos, and handoff entries. The closeout packet gathers all of it in one place, ready to file or hand to an inspector.
